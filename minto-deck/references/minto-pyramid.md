# The Pyramid Principle applied to decks

Working summary of Barbara Minto's method in the skill author's own words.
Terminology follows the English edition. Use this file to build the storyline
in Stage 1 and to judge a deck in Audit mode. It is not a substitute for the
book; add further sources as separate files in `references/`.

## 1. The core idea

The audience holds a question. The deck answers it first and then proves the
answer. Everything below the answer exists only because the answer raised a
question in the audience's mind ("Why?", "How?", "Why do you say that?"), and
each lower level answers exactly that question.

Consequences for a deck:

- The main answer (governing thought, Kernbotschaft) appears before the
  evidence, normally on slide 2.
- Every slide title is a statement the audience can agree or disagree with.
- The structure is a pyramid: one apex claim, a key line of three to five
  supporting claims, and evidence under each. A deck flattens this pyramid into
  a sequence: key line claims become sections, supporting claims become slides.

## 2. The opening: SCQA

The opening tells the audience where the deck starts and why the question
matters. It uses only material the audience already accepts.

- **Situation**: the stable, undisputed starting point. Nobody in the room will
  object to it.
- **Complication**: what changed, what went wrong, what is now at stake. The
  complication turns the situation into a problem.
- **Question**: the question the complication raises. Often implicit.
- **Answer**: the governing thought.

Order variants, chosen by the audience's mood and prior knowledge:

| Variant | Order | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | S, C, A | audience needs the context |
| Direct | A, S, C | audience wants the answer first, context afterwards |
| Concerned | C, S, A | the problem is urgent and known |
| Questioning | Q, S, C, A | the audience arrives with the question already formed |

Keep the opening as short as the audience allows. One to three slides is the
norm. If everyone in the room shares the context, start with the complication or
the answer. An opening that restates what the audience knows costs attention and
signals that the presenter does not know the audience.

## 3. The three rules of a pyramid

Every grouping in the deck satisfies all three:

1. **Summary rule.** The idea above a group summarises the ideas below it. It is
   an inference, not a container label. "Three risks" is a label; "Three risks
   are manageable within the current budget" is a summary.
2. **Same-kind rule.** Ideas in a group are of the same kind. They answer the
   same question from the level above and can be described by one plural noun:
   reasons, steps, options, findings, risks. Mixed kinds signal a hidden
   grouping or a missing idea.
3. **Order rule.** Ideas in a group follow one logical order, and the order is
   visible in the wording.

## 4. Vertical logic: the question-answer dialogue

Each statement raises a question. The level below answers it.

- A claim of fact raises "Why do you say that?" and needs evidence.
- A recommendation raises "Why?" or "How?" and needs reasons or steps.
- A finding raises "So what?" and needs an implication.

Test every title-to-body relation this way: write the question the title
raises, then check whether the body answers that question and nothing else.
Material that answers a different question belongs on another slide or in the
backup.

## 5. Horizontal logic: deduction or induction

A group of peers is argued in one of two ways; never both at once.

- **Deductive**: a chain. Major premise, minor premise, conclusion. Each step
  depends on the previous one. The summary above states the conclusion
  ("therefore…"). Keep chains short; more than four steps lose the audience.
- **Inductive**: a set. Several independent ideas of the same kind support one
  inference. The summary above states what their similarity implies ("because of
  these…").

For the key line prefer induction. Three independent reasons are easier to hold
in mind than a four-step chain, and the audience can challenge one reason
without the whole argument collapsing. Use deduction lower down, where a short
chain explains a mechanism.

## 6. The logical orders

Inductive groups use one of three orders. Deductive groups use the chain order.

- **Time (chronological)**: steps of a process, phases, cause before effect.
- **Structure**: parts of a whole, regions, business units, components.
- **Degree (ranking)**: by importance, size, urgency, impact. State the
  criterion.

The order must be recognisable from the titles. If the audience cannot tell
why slide 7 follows slide 6, the order is missing or mixed.

## 7. Summaries are insights, not counts

A title or management-summary line that only counts its children ("There are
four reasons", "We analysed five areas") says nothing. Replace it with the idea
the children share:

- For action ideas (steps, recommendations): state the effect of carrying out
  the actions together. "Three changes cut onboarding from six weeks to two."
- For situation ideas (findings, facts, risks): state what their similarity
  implies. "All three delays trace back to one approval step."

If no such sentence can be written, the group is probably not a group. Regroup.

## 8. MECE

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. Check the two separately.

- **Exclusive**: no two peers describe the same thing. Overlap shows up as the
  same evidence used twice, or as two titles that could be merged without loss.
- **Exhaustive**: together the peers cover the scope the parent defines. This is
  only testable when the parent names the scope. "Costs" is not a scope; "the
  three cost blocks of the service desk" is.

Group size: three to five peers hold. Beyond five, look for a category that
groups them; the category becomes the new peer, the members move one level down.

## 9. The key line

The key line is the first set of claims under the governing thought. In a deck
it is the section structure, the agenda, and the body of the management summary,
in the same wording each time. Write the key line before anything else; if it
holds, the deck holds.

## 10. Problem definition before structure

When the deck recommends something, the recommendation answers a defined
problem. Define it before building the pyramid:

- **R1**: the current result the audience does not want.
- **R2**: the result the audience wants instead.
- The question is how to get from R1 to R2; the governing thought is the answer.

An issue tree breaks that question into sub-questions (MECE), each answered by
analysis. Use it to derive the key line when the material is raw. Do not force
it on a status report or a faithful summary of a given source.

## 11. Tests to run on every storyline

1. **Read-through**: titles only, in order, aloud if possible. Do they make the
   argument on their own? Where does the listener ask "wait, why?"
2. **Disagreement test**: can a reasonable person disagree with this title? If
   not, it is a label or a platitude.
3. **One-question test**: does the body answer exactly the question the title
   raises?
4. **Plural-noun test**: can each peer set be named with one plural noun?
5. **Order test**: can the order of each peer set be named (time, structure,
   degree, chain)?
6. **Summary test**: does each parent state an insight rather than a count?
7. **Scope test**: does each parent name the universe its children exhaust?

## 12. Frequent failures and the fix

| Failure | Looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Buried answer | governing thought on the last slide | move it to slide 2, keep the ending as restatement |
| Topic titles | "Current situation", "Next steps" | write the claim the slide proves |
| Container summary | "Three findings" | state what the three findings mean together |
| Mixed kinds | two reasons and one step in one set | split or regroup; find the missing idea |
| Mixed orders | ranking on slides 4 and 5, time on 6 | choose one order per set |
| Long deduction | five "therefore" steps at the key line | convert to an inductive set of three |
| Context dump | four slides of situation | cut to what the audience does not know |
| Hidden scope | "risks" without saying which universe | name the scope in the parent |
| Repeated takeaway | two adjacent slides with the same claim | merge, or sharpen the second into its own claim |
