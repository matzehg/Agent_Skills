# Memo mode: answer-first prose

A memo is the pyramid written out. The governing thought leads, the key-line
claims are the section headings, each paragraph proves its heading. The method
is the same as for a deck; this file holds what differs: memo types, the
storyline format for the gate, the writing rules, how to derive a thesis from
source material, and how statements are attributed. Read `minto-pyramid.md`
first; it carries the rules this file applies.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Memo type, audience and their question named; attribution setting settled
- [ ] Governing thought in the first sentence or title, as a claim someone could disagree with; marked "derived" when the user did not supply it
- [ ] SCQA opening with all four lines in the storyline (situation, complication, question, answer = governing thought verbatim); only what the reader does not know; variant named
- [ ] Key line of two to five full-sentence claims (three to five the norm), weighted by relevance and evidence quality, grouped by theme or argument, never by source order; one order, criterion named; evidence mark per claim
- [ ] Register with a "Sets" line: each set one plural noun, members in fixed order
- [ ] Dissent placed under the claim it concerns; implications for the reader planned where the type requires them
- [ ] Every heading a full sentence of up to roughly 20 words; first sentence of every section and paragraph a claim; at most three sentences per paragraph; three or more parallel items or questions as a list
- [ ] Closing restates the governing thought; ask or next steps where the type requires
- [ ] Evidence with unit, period and source; attribution as settled; placeholders marked and listed; estimates, user assumptions and unsupported figures stay marked as such, never promoted to fact
- [ ] Source material (sessions, documents, speakers) in an annex, not in the body
- [ ] Length within budget; no framework labels in the text
- [ ] Storyline approved before the memo is written, as SKILL.md "The gate" defines it; short prose under roughly 300 words is delivered directly

Detail follows; the checklist is what gets ticked before delivery.

## 0. Stages in detail

The short form of the three stages is in `SKILL.md`, Memo mode. The full
procedure follows; the gate is defined in `SKILL.md`, section "The gate".

### 0.1 Stage 0: briefing

Take the items below from the request, the material and the conversation; ask
only for one that is missing and would change the storyline, at most three
questions at once.

1. Audience and their question: who reads this and what they must understand,
   decide or do afterwards. A mixed audience (internal and external readers)
   lowers the density of internal references and raises the attribution bar.
2. Memo type: management summary, briefing note (Vermerk), event or meeting
   report, decision memo, status note. `memo-format.md` lists the mandatory
   elements per type.
3. Governing thought the user already has. If none, derive one from the
   material and label it as derived; the gate exists so the user can replace it.
4. Length budget (words or pages) and destination: chat reply, a doc, a file.
5. Material and sources: what may be used, what must not be invented, how
   statements are attributed. For a report on talks or meetings, settle whether
   speakers are named, named by role, or not at all (`memo-format.md` section 5);
   the "none" setting applies only when the user or the material names an
   explicit agreement such as the Chatham House Rule.
6. Whether a deck follows. If yes, build the storyline so that it carries over.

### 0.2 Stage 1: storyline

Deliver, in the format in `memo-format.md` section 2: the governing thought in
one sentence; the opening as all four SCQA lines, situation and complication
as one sentence each, the question the complication raises, and the answer as
the governing thought verbatim, so the reader can check at the gate that the
answer answers the question; the key line of two to five claims (three to five is the norm) as full
sentences, ordered by theme or argument, never by the order of the source
material, each with an evidence mark (available, missing or placeholder, and
the source); the order and its criterion; where dissent between sources sits
and under which claim it will be argued; what the memo says follows for the
reader, when the memo type has that element; the attribution setting;
assumptions and open points; a short register when the memo names processes,
bodies, roles, systems or options that must stay consistent, with a "Sets"
line that names each set by one plural noun and lists its members in fixed
order (the plural-noun test: an institute does not belong in a set of
processes). Run the read-through test on the key line. End with one approval
question and stop, as "The gate" defines it; short prose follows the rule
below instead.

### 0.3 Stage 2: the memo

Write from the approved storyline, in this order:

1. **Lead.** The governing thought, with the SCQA opening before or after it as
   the chosen variant says. Keep only what the reader does not already know.
2. **Sections.** One per key-line claim. Heading = the claim as a full sentence
   in the storyline's wording, up to roughly 20 words; a longer claim gets a
   short-form heading and appears in full as the section's first sentence. The
   first sentence of each section restates or sharpens the claim; the rest
   proves it. One idea per paragraph, the first sentence of a paragraph is its
   claim, at most three sentences per paragraph; three or more parallel items
   or questions inside a paragraph become a list. Dissent is argued under the
   claim it concerns, as the storyline placed it.
3. **Implications.** When the memo type calls for it (event or meeting report,
   status note, thesis of a day): what follows for the reader or the reader's
   groups, the steps that were announced, and the questions that stayed open.
   Claims and evidence from the sections only; nothing new.
4. **Closing.** Governing thought restated with the confidence the evidence
   earns, then the ask or next steps when the memo type has one.
5. **Evidence.** Numbers with unit and period, named sources, attribution as
   settled in Stage 0. Unknowns as `[PLACEHOLDER: what is needed]`, listed
   under the closing. Source material in an annex (session table, list of
   documents) when the type calls for it, never in the body.
6. **Consistency pass** against the register and its sets; terms per
   `glossary.md`.
7. **Self-check** against the quick checklist in `memo-format.md` and checks
   A to D of `audit-checklist.md`, reading "heading" for "title".

No framework labels in the text: the lead is the situation, not "Situation:".
Lists for three or more parallel items, tables for items compared on several
attributes, prose for everything else. Keep the length budget. When the memo
runs over, narrow claims and shorten their evidence together, so that no claim
stands with less support than it had; a key-line claim may become narrower,
never disappear; conditions, counter-positions, attributions and the
limitations of the evidence survive every cut (memo-format.md section 3).


## 1. Memo types and mandatory elements

Identify the type first; the completeness check needs a target.

| Memo type | Mandatory elements | Typical length |
|---|---|---|
| **Management summary** (Zusammenfassung für die Leitung) | governing thought as first line; key line as three to five sentences, bold or as a list; the ask; key numbers that make the claim concrete; stands alone without the underlying deck or report | 150 to 300 words, one page at most |
| **Briefing note** (Vermerk, Sprechzettel in prose) | Betreff; purpose (information, decision, signature); Votum or governing thought first; Anlass (why now, who asked); Sachverhalt as agreed facts; Bewertung with risks and alternatives considered; next steps with owners and dates; attachments listed | one to two pages |
| **Event or meeting report** (Veranstaltungsbericht, Sitzungsbericht, Tagungsbericht) | thesis of the event in the lead; what it means for the reader's organisation or decision; key line by theme, not by session; dissent and open questions named; attribution as settled; session or agenda table with speakers, format, duration and one-sentence message in an annex | 600 to 1,500 words plus annex |
| **Decision memo** (Entscheidungsvermerk) | problem as R1 (today) and R2 (wanted); options with explicit criteria, shown before the detail of the recommended one; recommendation marked; cost, benefit, risk with period and owner; the decision worded as a proposal; consequence of no decision; next steps | one to three pages |
| **Status note** (Statusnotiz) | overall status in one sentence; planned versus achieved; deviations with cause; decisions or support needed; next period; risks that changed | half a page to one page |
| **Thesis of a day** (These des Tages; the shortest form of the event report) | one governing thought; key line of two to five claims; one sentence of evidence per claim; attribution and dissent kept; nothing else; delivered as short prose without storyline or mode label | 100 to 250 words |

Elements every type needs: a governing thought before the evidence, full-sentence
headings, a visible key line in the same wording in lead, headings and closing,
sources on external numbers, consistent terminology from the register.

## 2. Stage 1 output format (the storyline)

Use this layout and nothing else. No body text yet.

```
# Memo storyline: <working title>

Audience: … | Their question: …
Memo type: … | Length budget: … words | Destination: chat / doc / file | Language: …
Material: … | Not available / placeholders: …
Attribution: named / role only / none (see section 5)
Deck follows: yes / no

Governing thought (<from user | derived>): <one sentence>

Opening (SCQA, variant: …)
- Situation: <one sentence the reader already accepts>
- Complication: <one sentence; what changed or is at stake>
- Question: <the question the complication raises for this reader>
- Answer: <the governing thought, verbatim>

Key line (order: <theme | time | structure | degree | chain>, criterion: …)
1. <claim as a full sentence>  [evidence: available | missing | placeholder; <source>]
2. <claim as a full sentence>  [evidence: …]
3. <claim as a full sentence>  [evidence: …]
(4., 5. only when the material needs them)

Dissent: <positions that contradict each other, with their sources; argued under claim n> | none

Implications for the reader (event or meeting report, status note, thesis of a day):
<one line per reader group or decision; "none for this type" otherwise>

Closing: <governing thought restated> | Ask / next steps: <… or "none for this type">

Register (only when names must stay consistent)
| Entity | Category | Definition / source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
Sets: <plural noun> = <members in fixed order>; one category per set (an institute
is not a process; a document is not a body), selection universe and criterion
where the set is a subset.

Annex planned: <session table | document list | none>

Assumptions
- …

Open points for the user
- …

Alternative key line (only if the material supports a second structure)
- …
```

Every key-line line is a complete sentence with subject and verb, in the memo
language. The headings in Stage 2 repeat these sentences verbatim, or in a
short form of up to roughly 20 words with the full claim as the section's
first sentence. The answer line repeats the governing thought word for word;
if it does not answer the question line, one of the two is wrong.

## 3. Writing rules

**Lead.** The first sentence is the governing thought, or the complication in
the "concerned" SCQA variant with the governing thought in the second sentence.
The lead carries the key line in compressed form when the memo exceeds roughly
500 words, so that a reader who stops after the lead still has the argument.
Two to four sentences. No "This memo summarises", no "In the following".

**Headings.** The key-line claims, verbatim, up to roughly 20 words. A longer
claim gets a short-form heading that keeps its verb and its point, and the
full claim becomes the section's first sentence. A heading is a sentence the
reader can disagree with. "Regulierung" is a topic; "Die Regulierungsdebatte
hat sich von Verboten zu Kontrollmaßnahmen verlagert" is a heading.
Sub-headings only when a section exceeds roughly 300 words, and then as claims
too.

**Paragraphs.** One idea each. The first sentence states the idea; the following
sentences prove or qualify it. At most three sentences; a fourth sentence is a
new paragraph or belongs in a list. Three or more parallel items or questions
inside a paragraph ("Drei Leitfragen bestimmen es: …? …? …?") become a list
under a one-sentence lead-in. Sentences under 25 words, plain words, the
reader's language.

**Order inside a section.** The paragraphs of a section form one peer set:
same kind, one order, visible in the wording (`minto-pyramid.md` sections 3
and 6). When a section mixes reasons and steps, split it.

**Lists and tables.** A list for three or more parallel items of the same kind,
each item one line, starting with the claim. A table for items compared on
several attributes (options against criteria, sessions with speaker, format
and message). Neither as a substitute for an argument: a list of topics is a
topic heading in disguise.

**Implications.** Where the memo type requires them (section 1), one short
paragraph per reader group or decision, each opening with what that group
should do or expect, followed by the announced next steps and the open
questions as a list. Everything here is drawn from the sections; a new claim
belongs in a section, not here.

**Closing.** The governing thought in the same words as the lead, with the
confidence the evidence earned. Then the ask, the next steps with owners and
dates, or the open questions. Nothing new is introduced here.

**Density by audience.** Internal readers accept abbreviations and internal
names; external readers get the full name on first use and no internal
references. A mixed audience is written for the external reader.

**Framework labels.** None in the text. SCQA, key line, governing thought,
MECE belong in the storyline and in the author's commentary.

**Length budget.** Agreed in Stage 0, enforced in Stage 2. When the text runs
over, cut examples before mechanisms, and narrow a claim together with its
evidence rather than leaving a strong claim on thin support. What never falls
to a cut: counter-positions, conditions, attributions, and the limitations of
the evidence; the key line stays, a claim in it may become narrower.

Every sentence on the right maps to a sentence in the source notes; nothing
is added. The claim is attributed ("laut Referent X", "nach seiner

established fact. A derived interpretation (what the talk means for the
reader) would be marked as derived and kept apart from what he said.

## 4. Deriving a thesis from source material

When the user supplies notes, transcripts or several talks and has no governing
thought, derive one. Label it "derived" in the storyline; the gate exists so the
user can replace it.

1. **Collect the claims.** For each source (talk, session, document) write the
   two to four claims it actually made, as full sentences, with the evidence it
   gave. Skip descriptions of what was discussed; keep what was asserted.
2. **Cluster by theme.** Group the claims across sources by what they are about,
   not by where they came from.
3. **Weigh, do not count.** A cluster's weight comes from its relevance to the
   reader's question and the quality of its evidence, not from how many
   sources repeat it. A deadline, a new finding or a substantial objection
   stated once can carry a key-line claim; a point five speakers repeated can
   be background. Mark single-source claims with their source in the evidence
   mark so the reader sees the base.
4. **Name each cluster with its insight.** The sentence the claims in the
   cluster share or add up to (`minto-pyramid.md` section 7). "Three talks
   addressed testing" is a count; "Testing replaces explainability as the basis
   for trust" is an insight. These sentences are the key-line candidates.
5. **Find the apex.** What do the cluster sentences prove together? Write it as
   one sentence the reader's organisation could act on or disagree with. It
   answers the reader's question (what does this mean for us), not the
   speakers' questions.
6. **Test.** Disagreement test: could a reasonable reader object? Scope test:
   do the clusters cover the material that matters to the reader? Order test:
   can the order of the clusters be named? Read-through: lead, key line,
   closing, in sequence.
7. **Record dissent.** Where sources contradict each other, the contradiction
   is a finding, not noise. It goes under the claim it concerns or in an
   "open questions" section, with both positions attributed as settled.
8. **Separate derived from stated.** The apex and the cluster sentences are
   the author's inference from what was said; label them "derived" in the
   storyline. Exclusions ("not X", "no longer Y") are allowed only when the
   material actually dealt with X or Y; otherwise the apex names what the
   material supports and stays silent on what it did not cover.

Fictional example, a one-day expert event on AI governance with four talks.
Stated in the material: talk 1 describes new regulatory duties for operators;
talk 2 argues that testing and audits, not explainability, give grounds for
trust; talk 3 lists procurement hurdles and contractual fixes; talk 4 says
industry invests where tenders demand compliance. Clusters: "regulation now
addresses operators directly", "trust is argued from tests and audits",
"procurement and contracts are where requirements become binding". Apex,
derived: "Aus den Vorträgen ergibt sich für Behörden unmittelbarer
Handlungsbedarf bei Beschaffung und Prüfverfahren." A reader can disagree,
three clusters prove it, the order is degree (from the frame to the lever).
Not allowed from this material: "…nicht in neuen Gesetzen", because no talk
addressed whether new laws are needed.

## 5. Attribution and confidentiality

Settle this in Stage 0 and write it into the storyline; it changes the text.

| Setting | Use when | In the text |
|---|---|---|
| **Named** | public event, speakers listed on the programme, the user confirms | name and organisation on first mention per section; quotes only when the material has them verbatim and the user allows quotes |
| **Role only** | internal meeting, workshop, panel under house rules | "a ministry representative", "the industry side"; no names, no organisations that identify a person |
| **None** (Chatham House Rule or an equivalent agreement) | only when the user or the material names such an agreement; a closed meeting or a transcript by itself does not establish one | claims without source; the annex lists the event and its format, not the speakers |

Default when the user has not said: role only, and an open point in the
storyline asking whether names may be used. Whatever the setting in the text,
the author's working version keeps the traceability: which statement comes
from which recording or document, and where sources contradict each other.
That record stays with the user and is not part of the delivered memo. Transcript uncertainties (a name
the recorder misheard, a first name without surname) are flagged in the open
points, not guessed. The user's own contribution, when the user spoke at the
event, is attributed like everyone else's and not weighted higher.

## 6. The gate

Defined once in `SKILL.md`, section "The gate"; this is the memo view of it.
End the Stage 1 reply with exactly one question: whether governing thought and
key line are approved, or which lines to change, and stop. A reply with
changes and an instruction to continue is an approval: apply, show the changed
lines, write the memo. A reply with changes only gets the changed lines and
the question again. "Approved" or equivalent: write the memo and say which
storyline version it follows.

Short prose (roughly 300 words or less, including the thesis of a day) has no
gate and no storyline in the reply: the checks run internally and the finished
text is delivered in the requested format, without mode label, headings or a
repeated conclusion unless asked for. Changes afterwards are applied to the
text directly.

## 7. Bridge to Build

| Memo element | Deck element |
|---|---|
| governing thought | governing thought; title of slide 2 (management summary) |
| SCQA lead | opening slides 1 to 3 |
| key-line claims (headings) with evidence marks | section titles; bullets of the management summary; evidence column of the ghost deck |
| dissent line | Q&A preparation; the slide under which the counter-position is shown |
| implications for the reader | "what the audience can do with it" slide; next-steps slide |
| first sentence of each paragraph | candidate slide title; proven by that paragraph's evidence |
| lists and tables | evidence slides (table, cards, chart by comparison type) |
| closing with ask | closing slide, decision slide or next steps |
| annex (session table, document list) | backup |
| register | register, unchanged |
| open questions and dissent | Q&A preparation; backup |

Start Build Stage 1 from the approved memo storyline, add evidence marks and
the timing skeleton, and gate again only on what changed. The memo is then the
handout or the pre-read of the deck.

## 8. Frequent failures and the fix

| Failure | Looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Recap | sections follow the agenda or the speakers | regroup by theme; sessions go to the annex |
| Buried thesis | the point appears in the last paragraph | move it to the first sentence; keep the ending as restatement |
| Topic headings | "Regulierung", "Ausblick" | write the claim the section proves |
| Counting lead | "Six sessions covered five themes" | state what the themes add up to |
| Quote collage | the memo strings together what people said | claims first; a quote only as evidence for a claim, attributed as settled |
| Speaker weighting | one talk gets a section because it was long | sections prove claims; a long talk may yield one sentence |
| Hidden dissent | contradictions smoothed into one position | name both positions and what remains open |
| Internal shorthand for external readers | abbreviations and project names unexplained | full name on first use; write for the external reader |
| Budget overrun by evidence | three examples per claim | one example per claim; the rest to the annex or cut |
