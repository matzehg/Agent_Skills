# Speaker notes (Sprechzettel)

Notes are prompts that let the presenter speak freely, not a script to read.
A script read aloud sounds flat and breaks when the audience interrupts. Notes
that are too thin leave the presenter guessing at the numbers. The structure
below holds both.

## Where they go

In the notes pane of the slide file, one block per slide, plain text with the
labels below as line starts. Never in a text box on the slide. For Markdown
outlines, under a `Notes:` line after each slide.

## Structure per slide

```
Key point: <the one sentence the audience must take from this slide>
Open with: <first sentence, spoken language, links to the previous slide>
Talk track:
- <prompt 1: the claim in the presenter's words>
- <prompt 2: the evidence to point at, with the number>
- <prompt 3: the implication or example>
Point at: <chart element, row, number the presenter should indicate>
Transition: <one sentence that leads to the next title>
Time: <minutes for this slide>
Watch out: <optional: sensitivities, what not to say, likely interruption>
```

Keep the block to roughly forty to sixty words per minute of slide time. A
two-minute slide gets a hundred words of notes at most. Longer notes become a
script.

Write "Open with" and "Transition" in spoken language, first person where the
presenter speaks for themselves ("Ich zeige Ihnen…", "Let me show you…").
Write the talk track as fragments the presenter completes.

## By slide type

| Slide | Notes emphasise |
|---|---|
| Title | who is in the room, the one sentence that frames the next 30 minutes, the ask in one line |
| SCQA opening | what the audience already knows (say it briefly, do not explain it), the complication as the reason everyone is here |
| Management summary | the governing thought in the presenter's words, the key line as the route through the deck, the ask; tell the audience they can stop here if they trust the argument |
| Section divider | one sentence: what this section proves and why it comes now |
| Evidence slide (chart, table) | where the number comes from, what surprised the team, what the audience will challenge; point at the highlighted element |
| Options or comparison | the criteria, why the recommended option wins, what the losing options would have cost |
| Decision | the decision in one sentence, what happens if nothing is decided, the minimum the presenter needs today |
| Next steps | owners and dates; who in the room is needed for what |
| Closing | restate the governing thought, then stop talking; the silence invites the discussion |
| Backup | which question this slide answers; one line, the presenter knows the rest |

## Delivery cues

Use sparingly, in parentheses: (pause), (click), (ask the room), (slow down),
(name the number). Omit them in the handout variant.

## Context adjustments

- Internal audience: shorter, candid, refer to shared history.
- External audience: prove before concluding, define terms on first use.
- Hostile or sceptical audience: put the strongest evidence first in the talk
  track, name the objection before they do.
- Recorded or async: tighter, more signposting, no room interaction.
- Co-presenters: name who speaks on each slide in the first line.

## Full script on request

When the user asks for a word-for-word script (rehearsal, non-native language,
regulatory hearing), write it as a separate document, one paragraph per slide,
around 120 to 140 spoken words per minute, and keep the notes pane as prompts.
Do not put the script in the notes pane.

## Language

Notes follow the deck language. For a German deck with English technical
terms, keep the terms as the slide shows them and gloss them once in the
notes. For bilingual rooms, add a one-line English summary at the end of each
block only when the user asks.
