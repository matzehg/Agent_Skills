# Reading decks, pre-reads and handouts

A reading deck is a document laid out in slide format and read without a
presenter: pre-read before a meeting, leave-behind after it, circulation to
people who were not in the room. Nancy Duarte's term for the form is
"slidedoc"; Garr Reynolds' term for the failed version is "slideument", the
dense slide that is neither a good document nor a good slide. This file turns
the distinction into rules for the skill's reading-first density mode and the
handout variant.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Every page stands alone; headline is a sentence; headlines in order form the argument
- [ ] 75 to 250 words per page; paragraphs lead with their point
- [ ] Evidence as number, unit and source on the page; no presenter cues
- [ ] Navigation: summary or contents, tracker or section markers, page numbers
- [ ] One highlight per page; visual language fixed once
- [ ] Converted from the presented deck without changing storyline or order; PDF exported; file named as handout

Detail follows; the checklist is what gets ticked before delivery.

## 1. When a reading deck is the right form

Use it when all three hold: the content is detailed enough that people need
time to absorb it; the presenter will not be there to explain it; and the
material benefits from pairing short prose with visuals rather than from
either alone. Typical cases: pre-reads for decision meetings, project
documentation, research findings, leave-behinds after a pitch.

Do not use it as the projected deck. A page that works when read fails when
projected: the audience reads instead of listening, the presenter competes
with the slide. The skill therefore keeps the two apart: the presented deck at
speaker-led density, the handout as a separate reading-first variant of the
same storyline.

## 2. The spectrum

Documents on one end (prose, few visuals, read linearly), presentations on the
other (visuals, few words, carried by a speaker). Reading decks sit in the
middle: skimmable, visual, self-contained, read non-linearly. Design decisions
follow from that position:

- The reader chooses the order. Every page must stand alone and still fit the
  whole.
- The reader skims first and reads second. Headlines and highlights carry the
  skim; body text carries the read.
- The reader has time. Density can be higher than on a projected slide, never
  as high as in a report.

## 3. Writing a reading deck

- One concept per page. If a page needs two headlines, it is two pages.
- The headline is a full sentence that states the page's point: the same
  action title the presented deck uses. Reading all headlines in order gives
  the argument; this is the read-through test applied to a document.
- Body text in short paragraphs or short lists, roughly 75 to 250 words per
  page. Below that the page is a slide; above it a report page.
- Lead each paragraph with its point. Supporting detail follows; a reader who
  stops after the first sentence of each paragraph still has the argument.
- Evidence in reader form: the number, its unit and its source on the page.
  Presenter cues ("point at", "pause", "ask the room") never appear.
- Define terms on first use; the reader cannot ask.
- Write for the furthest reader: the person who forwards the deck to someone
  who has no context at all.

## 4. Designing a reading deck

- Visual language fixed once: one grid, one type system, one colour system,
  one icon set, one way to mark emphasis. Repeated on every page so that the
  reader learns it on page one and stops noticing it.
- Grid with generous margins; text columns no wider than roughly 70 to 80
  characters so lines stay readable.
- Type for reading: body 11 to 14 pt, comfortable leading, serif or sans
  both acceptable; headlines 18 to 22 pt. Hierarchy by size and weight, three
  levels at most.
- Whitespace as structure: it separates the page's parts and gives the eye a
  resting place. Dense is not the same as full.
- Visuals paired with text, not instead of it: the chart proves, the paragraph
  explains what it proves. Captions carry source and unit.
- Navigation for non-linear reading: a table of contents or executive summary
  page, section markers or a tracker on every page, page numbers, cross-
  references by page number plus short title.
- Highlighting that survives skimming: a bold lead sentence, a marked number,
  a callout box. One highlight per page carries the point; several compete.
- Sources, footnotes and references on the page. A reader without the
  presenter has nobody else to ask.

## 5. Delivering a reading deck

- Before the meeting as pre-read: the deck carries the full argument; the
  meeting is for questions and the decision. Say in the invitation what the
  reader should come prepared to decide.
- During the meeting as reference: the presented deck is short; the reading
  deck lies on the table or is linked, so detail is available without being
  projected.
- After the meeting as leave-behind: the reading deck travels; the presented
  deck without its speaker does not.
- Name the file so it is never mistaken for the presented deck.

## 6. Converting a presented deck into the handout variant

This is what the skill's handout step does; the rules above are its target.

1. Same storyline, same headlines, same order. No new pages except an
   executive summary page if the presented deck relied on the presenter for
   it, and a contents page for decks over about fifteen pages.
2. Per page: the key point from the speaker notes becomes the lead sentence
   of a short body text; the evidence prompt becomes a sentence with number,
   unit and source. Delivery cues are dropped.
3. Type one step smaller than the presented deck, within the ranges above;
   layouts may tighten but keep the grid.
4. Sources and footnotes added or expanded on every page.
5. Charts keep their descriptive titles; the headline above carries the
   message.
6. Export as PDF in addition to the editable file.
7. Read the whole document once as a reader would: skim headlines, then read
   two pages in full. Fix what the skim does not carry.

## 7. Audit checks for reading decks

- Headline on every page is a sentence; headlines in sequence form the
  argument.
- Every page understandable alone; terms defined; no reference to "as I said".
- No presenter cues anywhere.
- Word count per page within range; paragraphs lead with their point.
- Sources on the page, not only in a notes pane.
- Navigation present: contents or summary, tracker or section markers, page
  numbers.
- One highlight per page.
